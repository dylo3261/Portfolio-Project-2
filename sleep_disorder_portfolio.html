Overview

The Problem

Sleep disorders including insomnia and sleep apnea affect a large portion of the population and are often underdiagnosed. My portfolio asks: can everyday lifestyle and health metrics predict whether someone has a sleep disorder?

Using the Sleep Health & Lifestyle dataset (374 records, 13 features), a Random Forest classifier was trained to distinguish between three outcomes: No disorder, Insomnia, and Sleep Apnea.

Feature Engineering

Preparing the Data

Raw data is rarely model-ready. Before training, several preprocessing decisions were made to ensure the classifier had clean, interpretable inputs.

Class Balance Checked

The three target classes were checked for balance before training. A severe imbalance would cause the model to default to the majority class. My results followed ~219 None, ~78 Sleep Apnea, ~77 Insomnia.

Label Encoding Applied

Categorical columns were Gender, Occupation, and BMI Category. They were converted to numeric values using LabelEncoder, since decision trees require numeric inputs.

Feature Removal Applied

Person ID was dropped (not a real feature). Blood Pressure was dropped because it was stored as a complex string format that would have required additional parsing and introduced noise.

Missing Values Handled

Rows where Sleep Disorder was blank were not errors. they represent patients with no diagnosed disorder. These were explicitly filled with 'None' to form the third class rather than being dropped.

Model

Random Forest Classifier

A Random Forest was chosen because it handles mixed feature types well, is robust to outliers, and provides feature importance scores that explain why it makes predictions.

The forest consists of 50 individual decision trees. Each tree is trained on a random sample of the data, and predictions are made by majority vote across all trees. This ensemble approach reduces the overfitting that plagues a single decision tree.

Data was split 80/20 into training and test sets. The model was fit only on training data, and accuracy was measured on the unseen test set. A random_state=42 seed is set on both the split and the classifier to ensure results are fully reproducible.

The model correctly classified most healthy patients, with just 1 false positive.

The main source of error is confusion between Insomnia and Sleep Apnea (6 misclassifications) which makes sense since both disorders affect sleep quality and duration in overlapping ways.

Key Takeaways

What This Project Found

  • 1 BMI is the strongest predictor by far. At 0.259 importance, weight category outweighs all other features, suggesting metabolic health is deeply related with sleep disorder risk.
  • 2 Occupation is the 2nd strongest predictor. With an importance of 0.170 likely because professions correlate with stress patterns, work hours, and physical activity that influence sleep.
  • 3 Insomnia and Sleep Apnea are frequently confused. These two classes account for nearly all misclassifications, likely because both conditions reduce sleep quality and duration in overlapping ways.
  • 4 89.3% accuracy from 10 simple lifestyle inputs suggesting that low-cost surveys or wearable data could serve as a useful first-pass screening tool before a truee clinical evaluation.
